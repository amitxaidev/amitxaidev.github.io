How to Write an ATS-Friendly Resume in 2026 (and a Free Builder With 12 Templates)

Recruiters skim for about 7 seconds, and software often reads it first. Here's how to pass both.

By Amit Singh · Compiled Canvas

You've applied to fifty jobs and heard nothing back. Your skills are probably fine. Your resume is losing in the first few seconds. An eye-tracking study by the job site Ladders found recruiters spend about seven seconds on a first look, and at many large companies an applicant tracking system (ATS) reads your resume before any human does.

I built a free resume builder with 12 ATS-friendly templates for freshers and experienced developers, and wrote the rules below into it. It checks your bullets as you type. Here's what it checks, and why.

Rule 1: Simple beats pretty

An ATS reads plain text. Two-column designs, text inside images and star-rating skill bars can come out scrambled. Use one column, normal fonts and real text. And use the exact words from the job post: if it says Spring Boot, don't write only Spring.

Rule 2: Write every bullet with the XYZ formula

Laszlo Bock, who led hiring at Google, shared a simple formula: accomplished X, as measured by Y, by doing Z.

  • Before: "Responsible for backend APIs."
  • After: "Cut API response time by 40%, from 500 ms to 300 ms, by adding Redis caching."

No exact number? Use a fair estimate, a count or a scale: users, requests per day, hours saved, bugs closed. Then start with a strong verb: built, cut, led, shipped.

Rule 3: Get the layout right

  • Header: name, city, phone, email, LinkedIn, GitHub. No photo or date of birth for big-tech roles.
  • Summary: two lines, with your years, your strongest skill and one result.
  • Experience: newest first, 3 to 5 XYZ bullets per job.
  • Skills: grouped into languages, frameworks and tools. No star ratings.
  • Length: one page if you have under about ten years of experience.

5 mistakes that get resumes rejected

  • Typos. Read it out loud, one line at a time.
  • Duties instead of results ("worked on", "involved in").
  • One resume for every job. Tweak the summary and skills each time.
  • Every tool you've ever touched. If you can't answer two questions on it, cut it.
  • A file called resume_final_FINAL2.docx. Send a PDF named with your name.

Fresher? Your projects are your experience

Put education near the top, then projects written with the same XYZ bullets. "Made a to-do app" is weak; "Built a to-do API in Spring Boot with JWT login, used by 200 people" is strong. Add checkable numbers like your LeetCode count and a GitHub link. If you're preparing for coding rounds too, the free DSA in Java course and its 352-problem practice sheet are a good place to start.
