Two Pointers in Java: 5 Must-Know Patterns | DSA

?What this episode covers

You have a sorted list of one million numbers and need two that add up to a target. Checking every pair is about half a trillion checks. With two pointers it's at most a million steps. This episode teaches the technique from zero, with pictures and worked examples.

We cover five patterns. Three work from both ends of the array: reversing it, checking a palindrome, and pair sum, also known as Two Sum II. Two use slow and fast pointers moving in the same direction: removing duplicates and moving zeroes to the end.

Pair sum comes down to one rule. If the sum is too big, move the right pointer back. If it's too small, move the left pointer forward. If it matches, you're done. That's O(n) time and O(1) extra space. We show why it works, compare it with brute force on a graph, and write it in Java.

◎What you'll be able to do

  • What two pointers are and when to use them
  • Three patterns from both ends: reverse, palindrome, pair sum
  • Two slow and fast patterns: remove duplicates, move zeroes
  • Why pair sum works, and why it is O(n)
  • Writing pair sum and move zeroes in Java

!The mistake to watch for

Pair sum with two pointers only works on a sorted list. Also use left < right, not left <= right, or both pointers can land on one box and you use a number twice.

First, what are two pointers?

A pointer is just an index. A number that says which box you're looking at. Two pointers means we look at two boxes at the same time, and move them in a smart way.

There are two kinds. In the first kind, the pointers start at both ends, and walk towards each other. In the second kind, both pointers start at the left, and walk in the same direction. One goes fast, one goes slow.

Think of two friends cleaning a long bookshelf. One starts at the left end, one starts at the right end. They walk towards each other, and when they meet in the middle, the job is done. Nobody walks the whole shelf.

Pattern one. Reverse an array.

Take one, two, three, four, five. Left points at the first box. Right points at the last box.

Swap them. Now it's five, two, three, four, one. Move left forward, and move right back.

Swap again. Two and four change places. Five, four, three, two, one.

Now both pointers are at the middle box. They've met, so we stop. Two swaps, and the array is reversed.

Pattern two. Is a word a palindrome? A palindrome reads the same from both ends. Like madam.

Left points at the first letter, right points at the last. M and M. Same. Move both in. A and A. Same. Now both pointers meet at D. So madam is a palindrome.

Now try mango. M and O. Different. We stop right there. Mango is not a palindrome. One check was enough.

Pattern three. Pair sum. This is the big one.

Imagine you're in a shop with exactly eighty-five rupees. You want to buy two items, and spend all of it. The prices are sorted: ten, twenty-five, thirty-five, fifty, sixty, eighty-five.

Left points at the cheapest item, ten. Right points at the costliest, eighty-five.

Ten plus eighty-five is ninety-five. Too much. The total is too big, so we need a smaller number. Move right back, to sixty.

Ten plus sixty is seventy. Too little. Now we need a bigger number. Move left forward, to twenty-five.

Twenty-five plus sixty is eighty-five. Exactly our budget. Found it!

So the rule is simple. If the sum is too big, move right back. If the sum is too small, move left forward. If it's equal, you're done.

Why does it work? Because the list is sorted. Moving left forward always makes the sum bigger. Moving right back always makes the sum smaller. So every step throws away one number we never need again.

Here's pair sum. Left starts at zero. Right starts at the last index.

While left is less than right, we add the two numbers.

If the sum equals the target, we found our pair.

If the sum is too small, left plus plus. If it's too big, right minus minus.

And here's move zeroes. Slow starts at zero. Fast walks every box in a for loop. If the number at fast is not zero, swap it with the number at slow, and move slow forward.

Both of them visit every box at most once. So the time is O of n. And they only use two extra variables, so the extra space is O of one.

Now, the one mistake everyone makes.

Pair sum with two pointers only works on a sorted list. If the list is not sorted, moving left forward might make the sum smaller, and you'll walk right past the answer.

So if the list isn't sorted, sort it first. Or use a hash map, which we'll learn later in this series.

One more small tip. Use left less than right, not less than or equal. Otherwise both pointers can land on the same box, and you'll use one number twice.
